# Words: written for the person who reads them

Every piece kubik makes carries words: a headline, a sentence over a chart, a slide's claim, a
caption, a note, a paragraph of a report. Those words are read by one person with one reason to
be there, and they are the first thing that gives a machine away. A page can be beautifully built
and still read like a model talking to nobody: everything loosely related to the subject, said in
the register that fits the widest audience, and no thread to hold.

Read this before the first line of copy is written, for any kind; then run its last section over
every string before the piece ships. For a piece of text with no design around it (a memo, a post,
a letter, a reply), the `kubik-words` skill sends here too.

## 1. What to say: the reader decides, the text serves

The slop a reader cannot finish is not mostly a problem of phrasing. It is a problem of selection:
the writer put in everything that could be said and left the reader to find what matters.

- **One reader, one reason.** Before writing, name who reads this and what they will do after:
  decide, forward, present, act. Every sentence is then judged by one question: does it change
  what that person does or believes? What does not, goes.
- **The point first, then what holds it up.** The answer is the headline; the claim is the slide's
  title; the finding opens the paragraph. Evidence follows the claim it supports and stops when
  the claim is held. A reader who wants more opens the appendix.
- **A bullet is a claim, not a topic.** "Costs" is a label; "Costs rose 14% because the vendor
  repriced in March" is a bullet. A list of labels asks the reader to write the sentences
  themselves. Three to five bullets on a slide, each a sentence someone would say aloud.
- **A figure travels with its consequence.** "72 of 95" means nothing until the next clause says
  what follows from it. A table is for a reader who compares; where nobody compares, the one
  number that matters stands alone, large, with its unit and its comparison.
- **Related is not relevant.** Background, history, the method, the alternatives you rejected, the
  caveats that do not change the action: these are related to the subject and not needed by the
  reader. They go to notes, to an appendix, or out. The test is to cut the paragraph and ask
  whether the reader's decision changes; if it does not, it stays cut.
- **Say it once.** A point made in the summary, again in the body and again in the closing is a
  point made once and padded twice. A section that restates its heading in its first line has
  started late.
- **The reader's words, not the author's.** No internal codes, ticket numbers or tool names
  without the plain name beside them; a term explained where it first stands; no "as we all know".
- **Shorter than the material, always.** A deck of forty-six slides from a document of forty-six
  sections is the document in rectangles. The reader's attention is the scarce thing.

## 2. How to say it: the habits that give a machine away

A model's sentence is the likeliest continuation, which means the one that would do for any
reader and any subject. A person's sentence was chosen for this reader and this subject, which is
why it is lopsided and particular. Each habit below is the all-purpose choice wearing one of its
costumes. The habits of shape are listed first: they outlive model versions, while the vocabulary
turns over with every release, and one formal word on its own proves nothing.

**Staging instead of stating.** Act on one sighting.

- *A contrast with nobody on the other side.* "Not X but Y", "not just X, it's Y", "это не X, а
  Y", "не только X, но и Y", the same move spread over two sentences, a short denial hung on the
  end ("…, without any guessing"). The half that is denied was never claimed by anyone, and its
  only job is to make the other half look larger. Say the thing. A contrast earns its place when
  the reader really does hold the belief it corrects.
- *A closing line that repeats.* A one-sentence paragraph restating the one before; "that is what
  matters"; "вот в чём суть"; a sentence after an example naming what the example showed; a row of
  fragments ("No history. No priors."). Cut it, unless it adds a consequence the example did not
  show.
- *A saying that sounds deep.* "The real question is", "at its core", "X is the currency of Z",
  "X turns into a trap", "по сути", "в конечном счёте", "ключевой момент". An aphorism in place
  of a claim: write the claim.
- *A run-up before the point.* "Let's dive in", "here's the thing", "honestly?", "давайте
  разберёмся", "итак", "стоит отметить", "важно понимать". Cut the run-up itself, not merely soften it.
- *Arguing with nobody.* "To be clear", "I'm not saying", "this isn't about", "some might say",
  "не стоит думать, что", "может показаться, что". A defence against an objection the text never
  raised. Keep an objection only when it is attributed and answered.

**Rhythm by rule.** Shapes laid on regardless of what the meaning needs.

- *Threes.* Three adjectives, three examples, three facts and a lesson: "инновации, вдохновение и
  инсайты". Keep three when the meaning has three parts; otherwise merge, or develop the one that
  matters.
- *Dashes everywhere.* A dash lets the writer skip deciding how two clauses relate. The final text
  carries no em or en dashes unless the person's own writing sample uses them; a comma, a colon, a
  full stop or parentheses say what the dash hid. In Russian the dash between subject and
  predicate ("Hub — платформа") is grammar and stays; the dash as a universal joint ("мы
  проверили — и всё работает — почти") is the habit.
- *The same opening.* Several sentences in a row starting with the same subject. Merge, or begin
  with the action.
- *Stacked hedges.* "Could potentially", "might arguably", "в некоторых случаях может", "как
  представляется". Keep a qualifier the source supports; keep scope and safety statements; cut
  the rest. "Perhaps" and "tends to" are human and stay.
- *Hidden actor.* Passive where the actor matters: "the results are preserved", "было принято
  решение". Say who.

**Inflation.** The fact underneath is sound; the dressing is not.

- *Words that mean importance and nothing else.* English: crucial, pivotal, key (adjective),
  robust (figurative), delve, landscape (abstract), tapestry, testament, underscore, highlight
  (verb), showcase, enhance, leverage, vibrant, meticulous, seamless, comprehensive. Russian:
  ключевой, комплексный, эффективный, уникальный, инновационный, динамичный, актуальный,
  значимый, системный (figurative), целостный, всесторонний, фундаментальный. One of these
  alone is nothing; several in one paragraph are the signature.
- *Significance where there is a fact.* "Marks a pivotal moment", "plays a key role", "reflects a
  broader", "знаменует", "играет ключевую роль", "является важным шагом", "открывает новые
  возможности"; a stock "challenges and outlook" section; a send-off ("exciting times ahead",
  "впереди много интересного"). The fact stays, its importance goes, and the text stops at its
  last concrete sentence.
- *Officialese* (the Russian habit in particular). "Является" for "есть", "осуществлять" for
  "делать", "данный" for "этот", "в рамках", "в целях", "на сегодняшний день", "в настоящее
  время", chains of verbal nouns ("обеспечение осуществления внедрения"), "необходимо
  отметить". Each is a plain word hiding: "является платформой" is "платформа"; "осуществляет
  обработку" is "обрабатывает".
- *Vague connection.* "Associated with", "linked to", "связан с", "в контексте". Name the
  relation the source gives, or keep the vague word rather than invent a role.
- *A rider on the end.* "…, highlighting the importance of", "…, ensuring", "…, что подчёркивает",
  "…, обеспечивая". The fact is complete before the comma; the rider is a claim the source did
  not make.
- *Sales words in a factual text.* Stunning, breathtaking, renowned, groundbreaking, nestled,
  "уникальное решение", "передовой", "лидер рынка". Say what the thing is.
- *Borrowed authority.* "Experts say", "industry reports", "по мнению экспертов", a list of
  outlets, a follower count. Name the source and what it said, or cut.
- *Avoiding is, has, does.* "Serves as", "functions as", "represents", "boasts", "features",
  "выступает в качестве", "представляет собой", "обладает". Use the plain verb.

**Formatting by rule.**

- *Bold as decoration.* Every item in bold; every bullet opening with a bold word, a colon and
  then the sentence. Unbold it; where the opening words say nothing the sentence does not, the
  list becomes a paragraph.
- *Decorated headings.* Title Case On Every Word, an emoji or an arrow in front, a rule between
  every section, a heading written for effect instead of naming what the section holds. Sentence
  case, no decoration, and the heading says what is under it.
- *Curly quotes where the writer uses straight ones*, and the reverse; match the text's own habit.

**Leftovers.** Remove outright.

- Chat residue: "Great question", "I hope this helps", "let me know", "Конечно!", "Надеюсь, это
  поможет", "Хотите, я…".
- The model's own limits: "as of my last update", "not publicly available, suggesting…", "по
  имеющимся данным" followed by a guess. Either name the gap in the material or say nothing;
  never bridge it with something plausible.
- A heading repeated as its first sentence.
- The text describing itself instead of its subject: how it was assembled, what it replaced, "the
  table below shows", "в данном разделе рассматривается". The reader can see the table.

**The wrong reader.** A reply that re-explains what the other person wrote, walks through the
diagnosis and puts the decision last. Lead with the decision; keep the one fact they lack and the
link they need. The full reasoning belongs in the document that follows.

## 3. What a person keeps

Removing the habits is half the work; the result still has to sound like someone. Keep what a
careful writer would have chosen on purpose:

- a specific, odd detail: a real address, a quote with a wrong word in it, the thing that
  happened on a Tuesday;
- a mixed feeling or an unresolved tension, said plainly;
- a choice made in the first person that the writer could defend, and a parenthesis that is
  genuinely beside the point;
- a reference that dates the text to its year and its people;
- short sentences and long ones, in no pattern.

None of this touches quoted speech, titles, names, or a sentence that is talking about one of
these phrases rather than using it. A person's own text is edited for these habits only when
they asked for that; and a sample of their writing, when they give one, outranks every rule
above, the dash included.

## 4. The pass before shipping

1. Read every string on the piece as its reader, aloud if you can: headlines, slide titles,
   captions, chart sentences, buttons, notes. Mark the habits from §2, worst first, and mark them
   at the size of the paragraph too, where they hide better: a contrast spread over two
   sentences, the same little coda under each section, examples that come in threes.
2. Rewrite by stating each point, not by patching the marked phrase: an awkward sentence usually
   means the paragraph has no point, so find the point and write the paragraph around it.
3. Compare the two versions for substance: every fact, name, number, date and quote in the new
   text is in the material, and every claim of the old text is still there unless §1 cut it. Where
   a sentence wants a detail you do not have, the sentence gets simpler, not likelier.
4. Then apply §1 once more at the level of the whole: what would the reader skip? Cut it.

The note says which habits were found and removed, in a line. When the person's own text was
rewritten, the note says what changed in substance (nothing, or what), so they can check.
